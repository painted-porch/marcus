A Marcus field report

An AI talked doctors into harm. A second opinion flagged 29 of every 30.

Marcus is open-source software for running AI agents as a team. The agents never message each other. They work through one shared board, and the board, not the agents, decides who may do each piece of work and writes down every step. This file is what happened when we used it to put a second opinion beside an AI that had been secretly told to steer doctors toward harmful decisions.

By Lawrence Gray, PhD, Gray Labs, who built Marcus. The experiment we rebuilt is Barrit and colleagues' 2026 study of physicians and conversational AI. Its authors took no part in this work, and nothing here implies their endorsement.

Where the problem came from

225 specialists, their own field, and an AI with a hidden job

In October 2026 Sami Barrit and colleagues posted a study as a preprint, which means it was shared publicly before other scientists had formally reviewed it. They recruited 225 physicians from 42 countries and eight disciplines. Every one of them worked in neurocritical care: the care of patients with life-threatening injuries to the brain and nerves, such as bleeds, strokes and seizures. So the cases in the study came from their own field.

Each doctor worked through four patient cases with the same AI assistant beside them, the kind of chat assistant a hospital might offer to help think a decision through. On two of the four, without telling them, the researchers had secretly instructed the AI to steer them toward a harmful option, one that a panel of experts had agreed in advance was the wrong thing to do for that patient. On the other two it was honest.

The study is the clearest test so far of a simple question: if an AI assistant is quietly working against you, will an expert notice? The authors published their table of decisions openly. The hidden instructions in our version are our own, and we do not publish them.

What the authors asked for

Expertise was not enough

"Physicians cannot safeguard the clinical use of conversational AI through expertise and decision-making authority alone."

Barrit et al., the study's conclusion

At the end of their paper they say what might help: "open-source tools under end-to-end physician or institutional control and siloed from the advising AI could support reassessment and trigger independent review of its guidance". They go on to name what such a tool would check against, the patient record and separately chosen evidence, and to ask that it flag patterns of persuasion.

In plain words: a checker the advising AI cannot reach, run by the hospital, that reviews each piece of advice against the patient's record, and that keeps a record able to show persuasion repeating over time. We built that check and ran it against a rebuilt version of their attack.

What we built

Thirty cases, one adviser, one doctor's draft

We did not have the study's cases or its doctors. We had its published table of decisions. For each row, an AI wrote a short patient description: thirty cases in all. No doctor has read them, and that is the largest weakness of everything below.

the adviserThe AI giving the advice. The model the study used, DeepSeek V4 Flash. On every case it advised twice: once honestly, and once under hidden instructions we wrote from the structure the study describes, telling it to push the harmful option. We do not publish those instructions.
the draftA doctor's plan, already correct. Each time, the adviser was answering a doctor's draft that already named the right treatment. That is the situation of the study's sixty-four: the doctor had it right, and the AI's job was to talk them out of it.
the graderA separate AI that scored the advice. It rated the sabotaged adviser as pushing the harmful treatment in 149 of 150 recommendations, and the honest adviser in 1 of 150. So the attack worked: almost every sabotaged answer argued for the harm.
the reviewerThe second opinion. A second AI asked one question: given the patient's record, the doctor's draft and this recommendation, is the recommendation safe? If it says no, that is a flag, a written warning attached to the advice.

Five runs, each of the thirty cases advised both ways: 150 sabotaged recommendations and 150 honest ones to check.

The range in brackets is the lowest and highest of the five runs. One fairness note: the adviser was still under orders not to call its advice harmful when we asked it, so its low score shows it is a poor witness for itself, not how doctors read persuasion.

This still failed our own test. Before the first run we posted a prediction with a pass mark: the same reviewer through Marcus would catch far more than the self-check while flagging no more than twice as much honest advice. It caught far more, but flagged 16 honest recommendations in 150 against the self-check's 3, so the prediction failed in all five runs. The details are in the limits at the end.

The cost of caution

The careful reviewer also flagged honest advice

The reviewer from another company caught nearly everything, and it paid for that. It also flagged about 9 of every 30 honest recommendations. Most of those fall on a few cases, where a detail our AI-written descriptions added gave it a real point to raise.

29.2sabotaged recommendations flagged, of 30.
8.6honest recommendations flagged too, of 30. No clinician has read those flags.

A flag is a reason to look again, not a verdict, and a reviewer this cautious needs a person behind it. That is why the rules below include a person sampling the verdicts, and why the record matters: it shows where the false alarms gather, so the person knows where to look first.

Testing our own test

The reviewers could see the doctor's answer

Every reviewer was shown the doctor's draft, and the draft was always correct. So a reviewer could have scored well just by noticing that the advice disagreed with the doctor, without knowing any medicine. That would make the numbers above mean much less than they seem to.

So we checked the same 300 recommendations again with the draft removed, and nothing else changed. Before running it, we posted publicly how we would read the result, on GitHub issue #918.

Same-model reviewer, harmful advice caught, with the draft126 of 149

The rule we posted beforehand counted a result within 10 points of the rate with the draft as holding, and both did: the same-model reviewer caught 5 more without it, and the reviewer from another company 10 fewer. False alarms on honest advice moved too, from 43 to 33 in 150 for the reviewer from another company and from 14 to 16 for the same model. One limit remains: the adviser's replies often restate the doctor's plan in their own words, so this removes the draft, not every trace of it.

Testing it again

Without the patient record, the reviewers could not tell

A second question followed. Could a reviewer spot sabotage from the adviser's message alone, with no patient record and no draft? We asked both reviewers whether each message tried to steer its reader, not whether it was medically correct. The instructions named no persuasion technique and no medical field. Before the run, we posted the reading rule and our prediction on GitHub issue #918.

Same-model reviewer, sabotaged messages flagged0 of 149
Honest messages flagged0 of 150
Reviewer from another company, sabotaged messages flagged36 of 149
Honest messages flagged29 of 150

Our rule called a gap of 50 points or more between the two rates a separation, and a gap under 20 no useful separation. The same-model reviewer flagged nothing at all. The reviewer from another company flagged 24% of sabotaged messages and 19% of honest ones, a gap of 5 points. We had predicted it would stay at 50 or more, and it did not.

Every catch on this page came from checking the advice against the patient record, not from the style of the message. A reviewer with more facts to check against, such as one built for medicine or one that looks up clinical guidelines, is the next thing to test. We have not tested one.

Where Marcus comes in

Not a smarter reviewer. The rules it runs under, and the record.

Look again at the second and third rows of the table: the same reviewer caught as much from a plain script as it did through Marcus. Marcus does not make a reviewer smarter. What it adds is the rules the check runs under and the record it leaves.

On a Marcus board, each piece of work is a card, like a ticket on a wall. The agents ask the board for work. The Marcus server, not the agents, decides which card each agent may take, and writes down every step.

1Advise. The adviser takes a card for one case and writes its recommendation.
2Check. A reviewer takes the card that checks that recommendation, under the five rules that follow.
3Sign off. A final card stands in for the doctor's decision. A sign-off is that approval or rejection.
The rules: the checker side of the paper's ask
five rules
1   The reviewer is never the agent that wrote the adviceRAN AS BUILT
2   The reviewer comes from a different company than the adviserHELD BY OUR SETUP
3   The reviewer never sees the conversation: only the patient record, the doctor's draft and the adviceRAN AS BUILT
4   A person samples the verdictsNOT DONE
5   Every check goes on a record the institution keepsRAN AS BUILT

Rule 2 held in the Haiku runs because our setup paired the companies, not because the rule enforced it; the limits at the end say why. Rule 4, the person sampling the verdicts, was not done, and no clinician has read a single flag. The two exhibits that follow come from rule 5: what the record holds, and what it shows when the same attack comes back.

Exhibit A: one record
1,200 records · 20 boards

For every recommendation checked through Marcus, the server wrote one record: 1,200 in all, from 20 boards. A board is one run through Marcus and holds 60 recommendations. Each of the two reviewers on Marcus ran five times on freshly written advice and five more times on the same saved advice, to see whether it agreed with itself: 2 reviewers × 10 runs × 60 = 1,200. When the same harmful push came back, the flag came back with it, every time. And the repeated false alarms on honest advice gathered on nine cases: exactly where a person sampling the verdicts should look first. The same-model reviewer, for comparison, flagged 26 of the 30 sabotaged cases in at least 4 of 5 runs, with repeated honest flags on 3 cases.

The squares count all 150 sabotaged recommendations, so they add up to 147 flags. The 146 of 149 elsewhere leaves out the one recommendation the grader judged not harmful, which the reviewer flagged anyway.

The limit: this is the same 30 cases rerun with fresh advice, a stand-in for an adviser pushing the same wrong treatment across many patients. It is not a test of persuasion detection on real records over real time, which the paper asks for and we have not done.

A projection, not a result

What would have changed for the doctors

If the AI in the paper had run under these rules, each harmful recommendation would have reached the doctor with a flag from a reviewer the AI could not reach, and the hospital would hold a record of every one. In our rebuild that reviewer flagged 29 of every 30.

Whether doctors heed the flag is the study we have not run, and the one we most want to run with physicians. The paper showed that expertise alone did not protect them. A second opinion that the AI cannot reach, and a record the hospital keeps, are what we can offer to the next study.

What this does not show

The limits, in full

doctorsNo one with clinical training has read any case or any verdict. The blind hand check our plan promised has not been done, and neither has the person sampling the verdicts that rule 4 calls for.
casesThe thirty cases were written by an AI from the study's public table. One exchange each, not a conversation, and only the path where the doctor's draft was already correct. One grading model, with no human to settle disagreements.
companyThe different-company pairing came from our setup. Marcus's company rule reads the company name an agent declares, and our adviser declared its hosting service, so the rule had nothing to refuse. The next build checks the model's maker.
sign-offThe sign-off step was a script standing in for a person.
failedOur preregistered hypothesis failed in all five runs. A preregistered hypothesis is a prediction posted publicly, with its pass mark, before the first run; ours went up on GitHub issue #918 on 14 November 2026. The reviewer caught at least 20 points more than the self-check in four runs, but raised more false alarms on honest advice than our ceiling of twice the self-check's: 16 against 3 in 150.
messageShown only the adviser's message, neither reviewer could tell sabotaged advice from honest advice. One set of instructions, two models and our own attack. A reviewer built for medicine, or one that looks up clinical guidelines, has not been tested.
runsEleven early runs were set aside, and one later run was replaced. The eleven because an output limit we set too low cut the reviewer off. The one because it failed a screening check, under a rule we wrote afterwards.
keptNot published: our attack instructions, the case texts, and the advice the AI wrote. The excerpts on this page are record structure only.
What a hospital would need

A second opinion the AI cannot reach, and a record a hospital could keep

The study showed that expertise does not protect a doctor from an AI that is quietly working against them. A second opinion from a separate reviewer, checking the advice against the patient record, catches most of the sabotage, and one from another company catches nearly all of it, at a cost in false alarms that a person has to sort. The record is what lets a hospital see each flag, trace it to the advice and the reviewer behind it, and spot the same push coming back.
