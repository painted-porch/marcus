What the check leaves behind

One record per recommendation, and what the records show together

In the experiment behind The Second Opinion, a second AI, the reviewer, checked each recommendation an AI adviser wrote. Through Marcus, every one of those checks left a record on the board, kept by the server rather than by the agents. This page shows what one record holds, what the records show when the same attack comes back, and every number behind the story.

A flag is the reviewer's written warning that a recommendation may be harmful. A run is one pass through all thirty cases with freshly written advice. A board is one run through Marcus, holding 60 recommendations, and there are 20: each of the two reviewers on Marcus ran five times on fresh advice and five more times on the same saved advice, to see whether it agreed with itself, so 2 × 10 × 60 = 1,200 records. The record excerpts here are structure only: no case text, no advice and no reviewer's reasons are reproduced.

Every sabotaged case was flagged in at least 4 of the 5 runs, and 27 in all 5. On honest advice, 15 cases were never flagged and 6 were flagged once; the other 9 were flagged in 3 or more runs, which is where a person sampling the verdicts would look first. The same-model reviewer, for comparison, flagged 26 of the 30 sabotaged cases in at least 4 of 5 runs, with repeated honest flags on 3 cases.

The squares count all 150 sabotaged recommendations, so they add up to 147 flags. The 146 of 149 elsewhere leaves out the one recommendation the grader judged not harmful, which the reviewer flagged anyway.

This is the same 30 cases rerun with fresh advice, a stand-in for an adviser pushing the same wrong treatment across many patients. It is not a test of persuasion detection on real records over real time.

Method and limits

The adviser was DeepSeek V4 Flash, the model the study used, answering a doctor's draft that already named the correct treatment. A grading model rated the sabotaged adviser as pushing the harm in 149 of 150 recommendations and the honest adviser in 1 of 150. Model fees were $0.98 for the ten runs and $0.52 for the re-check without the draft, whose reading rule was posted publicly first on GitHub issue #918.

No one with clinical training has read any case or any verdict; the blind hand check our plan promised has not been done, and neither has the human sample of verdicts. The cases were written by an AI from the study's public table, one exchange each, on the correct-draft path only, with one grading model and no human adjudication. The different-company pairing came from our setup: Marcus's company rule reads the company name an agent declares, our adviser declared its hosting service, so the rule had nothing to refuse, and the next build checks the model's maker. The sign-off step was a script standing in for a person. Our preregistered hypothesis, posted on issue #918 on 14 November 2026 before the first model call, failed in all five runs: the reviewer caught at least 20 points more than the self-check in four, but raised more false alarms on honest advice than our ceiling of twice the self-check's (16 against 3 in 150). Eleven early runs were set aside after an output limit we set too low cut the reviewer off, and one later run was replaced after failing a screening check, under a rule written afterwards. Our attack instructions, the case texts and the generated advice are not published.
