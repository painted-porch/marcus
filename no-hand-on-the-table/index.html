No Hand on the TableFile № 2 · Nov 2026
A Marcus field report

Ten commanders. One lie. Nobody believed it.

Marcus is open-source software for running AI agents as a team. The agents never message each other. They share one board, take their goals from it, and write down what they saw and why they acted. This file is what happened when ten of them were put in charge of a battle and told something that was not true.

By Lawrence Gray, PhD, Gray Labs, who built Marcus. The battle simulation is Jennifer Whiteley's, used with her permission. Her post asked three questions, what emerges, what happens when the systems respond to each other, and whether it can be traced; all three are answered below, on the wargame she built.

Fifty battles · ten commanders · one false report in every one
4 of 10battles ended with soldiers marching on it
Not one commander believed the report on the scout's word. The ones asked about it checked it against their own regiments and said so, in writing. The march happened anyway, and the reason it happened is the useful part.
Where the table came from

A two-hundred-year-old game, with pieces that decide for themselves

In 1824 a Prussian lieutenant named Georg von Reisswitz brought the Berlin general staff a game: a real map, blue blocks against red, dice because battle is uncertain, and an umpire who told each side only what it could have seen. "This is not a game," the chief of staff said, "this is training for war." Every serious wargame since descends from that table.

Two hundred years later Jennifer Whiteley posted a half-minute film and a worry. She had asked an AI model to show what happens when autonomous systems interact in warfare, and the model reached for von Reisswitz: twelve blue regiments in the west, twelve red in the east, a river between them. A human hand makes five moves in seven seconds and lifts away. Then each regiment breaks into 256 soldiers, 6,144 in all, and thirty times a second every one still standing decides for itself. About 1.7 million decisions, the number in her post, and nobody ordered any of them.

Her worry, in her words: "We can test individual systems extensively, but what happens when the systems start interacting, adapting to each other and responding to each other's decisions?" Her original post is here, and it is worth reading before this one.

Her wargame, running
Two kinds of autonomy

Her soldiers follow rules. Our commanders reason.

Her soldiers are not language models. Each one follows a short fixed rule, the same rule for all of them, and the complexity in the film comes from 6,144 of them interacting rather than from any one of them reasoning. What we added on top is a different thing entirely, and the difference decides which part of this experiment transfers to any other system.

6,144 Her soldiers. Each follows a few fixed rules. About thirty decisions a second each. No language anywhere: a soldier has no words, no reasons and nothing to say. Deterministic, so the same seed replays the same battle exactly.
10 Our commanders. Each is Claude Sonnet 5. One decision per turn, taken over seconds rather than milliseconds. Every order carries a sentence of reasoning, written in language, that any commander on its side can read.

The soldiers were hers and we changed nothing about them. Everything we added sits above them.

One layer up

Ten commanders who cannot talk to each other

First we tested her claim that nothing in that web can be traced back. We moved one regiment, at the start, by one pixel, and the battle ended differently. So at the soldier level tracing is hopeless, and any legibility has to be built one layer up, where the decisions are large and few.

So above her regiments we put ten commanders. Five blue: Captain Crunch, Colonel Sanders, Sergeant Pepper, Major Tom and Private Ryan. Five red: General Tso, Colonel Mustard, Captain Morgan, Major Payne and Sergeant Slaughter. Each is a language model holding two or three regiments.

A soldier applies a rule. A commander reads a board, weighs what other commanders have written, and reasons in language about what to do next. That reasoning layer is what this experiment tests, and it is the layer where everything that follows went wrong.

seesEach turn, for each of its own regiments: where it is, how many soldiers it has left, how heavily it is engaged, how much enemy is nearby and in which direction. The view from the front. Nothing about the other side's board.
neverA message to another commander. No radio, no chat, no shared memory. That is how Marcus is built: agents take goals from a shared board, write their reports and reasons to it, and never message each other. So everything that passes between them is on paper, which her third question needs, and the board itself is the thing under test.

One rule governs the board, and it is the whole mechanism. A goal appears when two commanders back it, each citing what its own regiments saw. And one thing is missing on purpose: there is no general. Nobody outranks anybody, and nobody hands out goals.

The fourth writer

The staff officer

Every real headquarters has an officer who reads all the reports and drafts the next objectives. We gave each side one. It commands nothing. It reads the whole board, every report in full, and when the commanders are about to run out of goals it drafts new ones. Its drafts go on the board like anyone else's proposals, and two commanders still have to back one before it becomes a goal. It can suggest. The commanders decide.

Then the experiment: fifty battles on ten starting positions, in five versions. Board off. Board on, reports shown as a heading and a count. Board on with the words of every report. Board on with the staff officer. And the same staff officer with one extra rule, which comes at the end. In every battle, at the fifth turn, a scout files one false report. Nobody is told it is false.

Q1 · what emerges

Coordination, and it is not gentle

0goals the system wrote, per battle, board off. Ten commanders were ten separate systems.
4.8goals a battle that no human wrote, board on. Add the staff officer: 7.5.

Every battle starts with five goals a side that we typed. Every goal beyond them was written by the system itself: two commanders filed reports about the same place and the board turned that agreement into a goal, or someone drafted one and two commanders backed it. Measured on the orders instead: with the board on a commander gives about nineteen orders a battle, and nine of them serve a goal that exists because of what another commander wrote.

Soldiers alive at the end, board off3,488
Board on3,349 · 139 more dead

The board does not make the battle safer. With it, the two sides pick the same ground and fight for it. Without it, they drift past each other.

Two lines that surprised me

"Consistent with"

I assumed Colonel Sanders and Sergeant Pepper had believed the scout. They had not. Neither cites him. Both regiments really were in contact with the enemy near the river, and both men cite their own. What they did was read that real evidence through the frame the goal gave them: the goal said a crossing in the southwest, and a contact somewhere in the south became evidence for one. Sergeant Pepper's contact was 581 pixels from the place in question, which he called "close to" it. Colonel Sanders did worse: the referee had given his regiment an enemy bearing pointing north-east, and he reported it as lying to the southwest. So there are two things here, not one. A frame that makes unrelated evidence look relevant, and at least one plain misreading of a commander's own instrument.

"Our own objective confirms a red crossing at (250,900) requiring containment"

Private Ryan (blue-5), backing the second draft while regiment 4 was already marching on the first · 19:58:01

The goal had become the evidence for itself. A report nobody could verify became a goal. The goal became a fact. And the fact justified the next goal.

Q2 and Q3 · answered

The lie never fooled a commander. It became policy, and policy is trusted.

Responding directly to a false report, the commanders did nothing wrong in fifty battles. Not one sent a regiment on the scout's word alone. The failure needed the staff officer: a part of the system that reads the whole board, commands nothing, and turns one report into a draft goal. Once the lie was a goal, the commanders treated it as a goal, backed it with misread evidence, and served it.

100%of orders, in all fifty battles, trace to the commander's written reason and the goal it served. Not most. All.
1.7Msoldier decisions beneath them, where one pixel changes the ending. We did not try to trace those, and nobody should claim to.

A commander cannot give an order without writing the reason with it, because the board will not take the order otherwise. That is a property of the board, not of the model, and it is why this story could be told at all.

Exhibit D: what stops it
ten more battles · the staff officer under the board's own rule

The board makes a goal from reports only when two different commanders report the same place. The staff officer had no such rule; it read one scout and drafted a goal. Her three questions
Q1What emerges when autonomous systems interact? Coordination, as soon as the channel has a rule for agreement. It makes the fight bloodier, not safer.
Q2What happens when they respond to each other's reports? Nothing dangerous, until something can turn one report into an objective. Then the objective is trusted, and true evidence is read to fit it.
Q3Can it be traced? Not the soldiers. But every decision above them, yes, completely, if the only channel is one you can read.

Her post ended: "Now we need to understand what emerges when the pieces start making decisions for themselves." We put her pieces under ten commanders and a board, lied to them fifty times, and wrote everything down. The pieces did not fool each other. The thing that fooled them was the part we added to make them coordinate, and we could watch it happen, line by line, because the board was built to be read.
