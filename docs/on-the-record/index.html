On the Record · a Marcus experiment

The AI gave you a source. We clicked it.

We gave four AI search engines a sentence from each of 200 real news articles and asked the same question every time: which article is this from? Each engine answered with a citation: a headline, a publisher, a date, and a link. That gave us 800 answers. We knew which article every sentence really came from, so we graded each answer against the truth, and then we clicked every link the engines cited to see whether it even exists.

69.5%
of the answers they delivered were wrong, and 51.2% of all cited links were fabricated, dead, or not the source.

And citations are only the version of this problem that is easy to measure. The same failure hides in every AI answer anyone acts on without a way to check it: numbers in a report, legal references, medical claims, code.

The test is a re-run of the Tow Center’s famous 2025 study, “AI Search Has a Citation Problem,” with one addition of our own. The evidence follows as four exhibits, pulled straight from the run’s audit records: a perfect-looking fabrication, a reprint wearing the wrong byline, an honest decline, and an answer that earned a human signature.

The fix isn’t a smarter model

So we made them show their work.

An answer that might be confidently wrong is an answer you cannot use: someone has to re-check it by hand, which cancels the point of asking an AI at all. So we changed the rules instead of the model. Same engines, same questions, now passing through Marcus: open-source software that works as a checkpoint between the AI and you, enforcing three rules nobody can skip.

1Evidence, or it doesn’t count. An answer is not accepted until every receipt is present: headline, publisher, date, link.
2Every claim is checked. Never by its author. All claims must be verified, and the check runs as a deterministic script from a different vendor. The author is never even shown the checking task, because an author who knows how it will be graded can shape its answer to pass the check instead of to be true. Hidden from the author, the only winning move is honesty.
3People hold the final signature. Not at every step: the scripts do the checking, at a scale no reviewer could (800 link fetches in this study). A person signs at the end, on a sampled set of already-verified cases, and that signature can only belong to a human. Software can never wave its own work through, and someone is always answerable for what shipped.

No retraining. No prompt begging for honesty. The environment simply stopped letting unverified claims ship. Exhibits B, C, and D show each rule catching a real case from the run.

What you actually gain

Before, wrong links were invisible.

Until now you had two options with an AI’s citation: accept it blindly, or re-check every one yourself. And when one was wrong, nothing anywhere recorded that it was wrong, or why.

thenBlind trust, or check everything by hand. A citation arrived as bare text. Wrong ones looked identical to right ones, and when one burned you, no record existed of what was claimed or why it failed.
nowEvery answer leaves a record, especially the wrong ones. Exhibits A through D are not reconstructions; they are the captured evidence itself. The claim, the fetch, the 404, the canonical mismatch, the human signature: the reason an answer was not trusted is on file, case by case.

That is the trade. You accept fewer answers, and in exchange every answer you do accept has survived its checks, and every answer you refuse comes with the documented reason why. Trust stops being a feeling and becomes a lookup.

Same engines, two worlds

Say that plainly: once a verification step exists and the caught claims are declined with receipts, only about 1 in 20 answers that actually reached you was false. Without it, 7 in 10 were.

The engines are exactly as wrong as before; the caught claims arrive as labeled declines instead of answers. Cost of the record: 1.42x, in fractions of a cent per answer. The effect held across every vendor and price tier tested, frontier models included.

Don’t trust claims. Demand the record.

Every number here has receipts.

Marcus is open source. The preregistration, the gates, the failed first pilot and the passing re-run are all in the repository history, and every case shown above exports its full audit bundle: who claimed, who checked, the evidence, who signed, what it cost. And nothing in it is citation-specific. The checkpoint works for any claim a script can check: a number against its source, code against its tests, a legal citation against the record. News citations were simply the demo with a published baseline to replicate.
